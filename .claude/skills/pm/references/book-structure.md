# How the Book of Forms is organised and meant to be used

Read this before deciding which form a request needs, whether a form may be tailored or replaced, or when
in the project a document is created and updated. It condenses the front matter, the introduction, the six
group introductions and what each form's prose says about its own process and its own life. `forms-index.md`
has the page of every form; this file says what the arrangement means.

Citations give both numbers: "PDF 31 (printed 20)". In the body PDF page = printed page + 11. The front
matter is roman: the introduction is PDF 10 (printed ix) and PDF 11 (printed x), the contents PDF 6 and 7
(printed v and vi); PDF 1 to 5 and 9 are cover, title pages, copyright and a blank. PMBOK 6 pages are
"printed (PDF)", offset 39.

## 1. Where things are

Introduction PDF 10 to 11 (ix to x). Group introductions: 1.0 Initiating PDF 12 (1); 2.0 Planning PDF 28 to 30
(17 to 19); 3.0 Executing PDF 174 to 175 (163 to 164); 4.0 Monitoring and Controlling PDF 200 to 201 (189 to
190); 5.0 Closing PDF 240 (229), shared with the start of 5.1; Agile PDF 250 (239), no 6.0 heading. The 66
forms: Initiating 4, Planning 40, Executing 7, Monitoring and Controlling 9, Closing 2, Agile 4.

## 2. Audience, purpose and how the author says to use the forms

- **Purpose** (PDF 10, printed ix): a companion to the PMBOK Guide, Sixth Edition, presenting its information
  as forms so a project manager can apply it to project documentation. The book "does not teach" project
  management or how to apply techniques; textbooks and classes do.
- **Audience** (PDF 10, printed ix): project managers first. Newcomers use the forms as a guide to collect and
  organize information; experienced managers as a template so every project collects consistent data. The
  secondary audience is the manager of project managers or a PMO, for a consistent, repeatable approach.
- **Tailoring is expected** (PDF 10, printed ix): projects are unique, so "some projects will require
  information in addition to what is presented in these forms; some will require less". Each form has a
  Tailoring Tips section; electronic PDF and .doc versions exist, the .doc being the one to adapt. The
  introductions repeat it: tailor "by editing, combining, or revising" (1.0, PDF 12, printed 1; a similar
  sentence ends the Planning intro, PDF 30, printed 19).
- **Not every form is needed** (PDF 11, printed x): "Use the forms you need, to the degree that you need them."
- **Samples** (PDF 11, printed x): no completed samples for every form because the book spans all industries,
  but "a few" are on the publisher's website. 2.3 and 2.18 print a worked sample instead of a blank (PDF 40,
  printed 29; PDF 91, printed 80).
- **New in this edition** (PDF 10, printed ix): Tailoring, Alignment, electronic versions, and the Agile
  section, whose forms "are not mentioned in the PMBOK Guide" but may help even without an agile method.
- **Order** (PDF 11, printed x): by Process Group, and inside a group "arranged sequentially as presented in
  the PMBOK Guide". Planning numbering therefore follows PMBOK chapter order 4, 5, 6 ... 13 (section 6).

**Anatomy of a form** (PDF 11, printed x), in this order: a description with a contents list (Planning forms
add where information comes from and goes to); Tailoring Tips; Alignment, the other forms that must agree
with this one (the introduction's example: duration estimates agree with the Assumption Log, activity
attributes and resource requirements, PDF 10, printed ix); a Description table of fields; the blank form.
2.17, 2.21 and 2.32 have no Tailoring Tips and no Alignment section (prose pages PDF 84 to 87, 99 to 101,
139 to 140); take the documents to compare from their receives-from and provides-to lists.

**What the tips and prose allow.** Combine the charter with the scope statement, or use a statement of work as
the charter (1.1, PDF 14, printed 3). Make the project management plan a shell linking to separate subsidiary
plans, or use a roadmap on a small project (2.1, PDF 32, printed 21). Keep milestones in the scheduling tool
(2.14, PDF 76, printed 65). Skip the acceptance form when deliverables are few (4.9, PDF 237, printed 226).
Fold the contract closeout into the project closeout when one contract drives the project (4.8, PDF 233,
printed 222). Use the probability and impact assessment only when no risk management plan holds the
definitions (2.34, PDF 148, printed 137). Add escalation, decision and lessons fields to status reports
(4.1, PDF 202, printed 191).

## 3. The groups and what each introduction says

**1.0 Initiating** (PDF 12, printed 1). Two processes: develop project charter, identify stakeholders. Intent
at least: authorize the project, identify objectives, define initial scope, obtain organizational commitment,
assign a project manager, identify stakeholders. The processes "can be revisited throughout the project for
validation and elaboration". Forms 1.1 to 1.4.

**2.0 Planning** (PDF 28 to 30, printed 17 to 19). 24 processes, from develop project management plan to "plan
stakeholder management" (the book's wording; PMBOK 6 says 13.2 "Plan Stakeholder Engagement"). Intent: scope,
a realistic schedule and budget, quality, resources, communications, risk, procurement and engagement, and to
"combine all the planning information into a project management plan and a set of project documents that are
cohesive and integrated". Three statements shape how to read the forms:

- "Planning is not a one-time event. It occurs throughout the project." Approved changes mean "many of the
  planning processes will need to be revisited and the documents revised and updated" (PDF 29, printed 18).
- "Many of the forms in this section provide information needed for other forms"; each description says
  where information is received from and goes to (PDF 29, printed 18). That is the receives-from and
  provides-to lists.
- Some forms "are not explicitly described in the PMBOK Guide" but are useful (PDF 30, printed 19), such as
  the roadmap 2.3 (PDF 39, printed 28).

The intro's list of forms is not the numbered list: it adds "Assumption log" (1.2), "Bottom-up cost estimating
worksheet" (a second table in 2.21, PDF 99, printed 88), "Roles and responsibilities", and "Procurement
strategy" twice (PDF 29 to 30, printed 18 to 19). Count forms from the contents.

**3.0 Executing** (PDF 174 to 175, printed 163 to 164). Ten processes, direct and manage project work through
manage stakeholder engagement. "The main work of the project is carried out and the majority of the funds are
expended"; the project manager decides, identifies issues, coordinates resources, reports progress and engages
stakeholders. The list also names "Form test and evaluation documents", and a footnote says tests, metrics and
measurements are product specific so "there is no template" (PDF 175, printed 164). That is not a form.

**4.0 Monitoring and Controlling** (PDF 200 to 201, printed 189 to 190). Review results against plan; "a
significant variance indicates the need for preventive actions, corrective actions, or change requests". The
text says 11 processes and lists 12 (PMBOK 6 Table 1-4, printed 25, PDF 64, shows 12). It runs "throughout the
project, from inception to closing"; "all variances are identified, and all change requests are processed
here. The product deliverables are also accepted" here.

**5.0 Closing** (PDF 240, printed 229). One process, close project or phase. Intent: close phases and the
project, document lessons learned and final results, archive project records, ensure the work to close
contracts is complete. Forms 5.1 and 5.2.

**Agile** (PDF 250, printed 239). Four forms that "can be tailored for use in any project, regardless of the
development method". Agile teams often use software or Kanban boards, so few forms come from office software.
The book maps each to a process and says it may take the place of its traditional partner:

| Form | Process | Book's statement (PDF 250, printed 239) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 6.1 Product Vision | Develop project charter | before a charter, or instead of it on smaller projects |
| 6.2 Backlog | Collect requirements | in place of or in addition to requirements documentation |
| 6.3 Release Plan | Plan schedule management | high-level schedule; may take the place of a schedule management plan |
| 6.4 Retrospective | Manage project knowledge | in place of or in parallel with the lessons learned register |

## 4. Plans, baselines, logs, reports: the book's own words

The book has no single taxonomy. The skill's `kind` column records page shape, not role; this is what each
form says about itself.

**Plans.** The project management plan 2.1 is "primarily comprised of all the subsidiary management plans and
the baselines" (PDF 31, printed 20). Its 11 subsidiary plans are exactly the forms that call themselves "part
of" or "a component of" it: change 2.2, scope 2.4, requirements 2.5, schedule 2.11, cost 2.19, quality 2.23,
resource 2.26, communications 2.30, risk 2.31, procurement 2.37, stakeholder engagement 2.40 (PDF 36, 41, 44,
67, 93, 106, 115, 129, 132, 158, 169; printed 25, 30, 33, 56, 82, 95, 104, 118, 121, 147, 158).

**Baselines.** The scope baseline is the approved WBS 2.9, WBS dictionary 2.10 and scope statement 2.8
together (PDF 60 and 63, printed 49 and 52). The cost baseline 2.22 is a time-phased budget and cumulative
cost curve that "may be called the performance measurement baseline" under earned value; it generally holds
contingency reserve and not management reserve, which sits above it inside the budget, unless company policy
differs (PDF 104, printed 93). The performance measurement baseline integrates scope, schedule and cost (2.1,
PDF 31, printed 20). A schedule baseline is named in 2.1 and as the receiver of 2.11 and 2.16 (PDF 31, 67, 81;
printed 20, 56, 70), but no form is identified as the schedule baseline; whether the 2.18 project schedule is
one is not stated in the book.

**Logs, registers and lists.** Titled as such: assumption log 1.2, stakeholder register 1.3, risk register
2.32, issue log 3.1, decision log 3.2, change log 3.4, lessons learned register 3.5. 1.2, 1.3, 3.1, 3.2, 3.4
and 3.5 call themselves "dynamic" (PDF 20, 23, 176, 179, 186, 189; printed 9, 12, 165, 168, 175, 178). The
activity list 2.12 and milestone list 2.14 are lists. 3.1 and 2.38 call themselves "a project document".

**Reports.** 4.1 and 4.2 are "examples of work performance reports" (PDF 202 and 207, printed 191 and 196); 4.6
is "work performance information" (PDF 224, printed 213). The risk report 2.33, contract closeout report 4.8,
lessons learned summary 5.1 and project closeout 5.2 are reports in the plain sense.

**Techniques and tools** (the book's label): stakeholder analysis 1.4 tool; RAM 2.25 data representation
tool; 2.34 and 2.35 tools; quality audit 3.6 technique; variance analysis 4.3 and earned value analysis 4.4
data analysis techniques; risk audit 4.5 tool; procurement audit 4.7 technique (PDF 26, 112, 148, 153, 192,
213, 217, 220, 229; printed 15, 101, 137, 142, 181, 202, 206, 209, 218). Worksheets 2.17 and 2.21 "help to
develop" estimates (PDF 84 and 99, printed 73 and 88).

**Authorizations.** The charter "formally authorizes" the project (PDF 13, printed 2); change request 3.3 goes
to the change control board (PDF 181, printed 170); 4.9 carries the accepting party's sign-off (PDF 237,
printed 226).

**Not PMBOK outputs**, by the book's own statement: roadmap 2.3, decision log 3.2 and the four Agile forms
(PDF 39, 179, 250; printed 28, 168, 239).

## 5. The life of a document across the project

The book has no single statement that a document is created in Planning, updated in Executing and Monitoring
and Controlling, and archived at Closing. Each form's prose says what that form does, and the group
introductions add a few facts. The pattern below is assembled from those statements.

**Group-level.** Initiating documents are revisited for validation and elaboration (PDF 12, printed 1).
Planning "occurs throughout the project" and approved changes send planning documents back for revision (PDF
29, printed 18). Monitoring and Controlling runs from inception to closing (PDF 200, printed 189). At Closing
the intent includes "archive project records" (PDF 240, printed 229), and lessons learned with their register
are saved "in a lessons learned repository", from a binder to a searchable database (PDF 241, printed 230).
Which other forms are archived, where, in what format and for how long is not stated in the book.

**Per-form statements of change.** Pages are PDF (printed).

| Pattern | Forms |
| --- | --- |

| Once, with a trigger to revisit | 2.27 on substantial turnover (120, 109); 2.6 once if scope is stable, evolving on adaptive projects (49, 38) |
| Started early, elaborated as information arrives | 2.9 high levels first, lower levels progressively (60, 49); 2.10 (64, 53); 2.13 (73, 62); 2.25 (112, 101); 2.35 (153, 142); 2.36 "continuously updated" (155, 144) |
| Refined throughout planning | 2.12 (70, 59); 2.16 (81, 70); 2.17 (85, 74); 2.18 (89, 78); 2.20, 2.21 "refined periodically" (96, 99; 85, 88); 2.24 (110, 99) |

"Not usually changed" is not frozen: approved changes send documents back for revision (PDF 29, printed 18),
and the change log 3.4 is "related to" the change management plan 2.2 (PDF 186, printed 175).

**Hand-offs between groups.** 4.1 and 4.2: new risks go to the risk register, new issues to the issue log (PDF
203, printed 192), decisions to the decision log and learning to the lessons learned register (PDF 202,
printed 191). 4.8 copies change ID, description and date from the change log (PDF 234, printed 223); 4.9 copies
requirements and criteria from the requirements documentation (PDF 237, printed 226); 5.2 takes verification
and validation from 4.9 (PDF 247, printed 236). 5.2 documents final performance against the charter's
objectives, including any not met (PDF 246, printed 235), aligned with "all components" of the project
management plan, product acceptance and 5.1. 2.32 feeds the lessons learned register and project closeout (PDF
139 to 140, printed 128 to 129); 2.14 feeds the closeout report (PDF 76, printed 65). A contract closes only
after disputes are resolved, the product accepted and final payments made (4.8, PDF 233, printed 222).

- **Names differ from PMBOK 6.** The book writes "11.7 Control Risks" (PDF 220, printed 209) where Table 1-4
  says "11.7 Monitor Risks", "6.4 Estimate Activity Duration" on 2.17 (PDF 85, printed 74), and "plan
  stakeholder management" in the 2.0 list. Cite the number and use the PMBOK 6 name in new text.
- **A form's group is not always its process's group.** The change log 3.4 is "used in" 4.6, a Monitoring and
  Controlling process, yet sits in Executing (PDF 186, printed 175); change request 3.3 may come "from almost
  any process involved in executing, monitoring, and controlling" (PDF 181, printed 170). The book does not
  say how a form's group was chosen; the examples fit the process where the form is first described.
- **Several forms serve several processes.** 4.3 names five (4.5, 4.7, 5.6, 6.6, 7.4); 4.4 names four (4.5,
  6.6, 7.4, 12.3).
- **Plans feed each other.** 1.1 provides information to 15 documents, from the stakeholder register to the
  stakeholder engagement plan (PDF 13, printed 2). Use each form's lists as the checklist for step 5 of
  `SKILL.md`.

## 7. What this means for the skill

1. A missing optional field is the author's tailoring, not an error in the book's terms (PDF 10, printed ix).
   This repo still keeps the printed fields and names any tailoring in the reading paragraph, per `SKILL.md`.
2. The book names four substitutions for agile work (section 3, PDF 250, printed 239); other swaps are not
   stated. Do not call the 2.18 project schedule the "schedule baseline": the book does not identify it.
3. A form the book calls "developed once" still changes through the route the book names: change request 3.3,
   change log 3.4 and change management plan 2.2 (PDF 181, 186, 36; printed 170, 175, 25). Team rule (memory,
   not the book): when constraints cannot all hold, the route is a change request (3.3) to the sponsor.
